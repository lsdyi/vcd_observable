Master's thesis · local model assessment

Interactive visualization of conditional distributions

A regression model can look convincing from far away and still fail in the exact neighborhood where a researcher needs it to work. This project turns that problem into something visible: choose a model, move through covariate space, and compare the model's local distribution with data-driven alternatives.

The central visual idea: compare the model-implied local distribution with a nonparametric estimate around the same conditional point.

From Global Fit To Local Fit

The thesis starts from a familiar diagnostic habit: compare a fitted model distribution with a data-driven distribution. In a global check, that comparison is made for the response as a whole. In the local version, the same comparison is made after asking where in covariate space we are standing.

Global fit check

Use when the first question is whether the model explains the overall response distribution.

  • Compare the model-implied marginal distribution with a histogram or KDE.
  • Every observation contributes to the check.
  • Useful as an overview, a first warning, or a sanity check before deeper diagnosis.

Local fit check

Use when the model may behave differently for different covariate profiles.

  • Choose a conditional point and weight nearby observations more heavily.
  • Compare the weighted model distribution with weighted nonparametric estimators.
  • Useful for finding where a model fits, where it fails, and how it might be refined.
The transformation is simple but powerful: global comparison becomes local comparison by replacing equal contribution from all observations with kernel weights around a selected covariate point.

The Method In One Pass

The dashboard keeps both sides of the analogy visible. The parametric side asks what the fitted model says the response should look like near the selected point. The nonparametric side asks what the nearby data say without trusting the model form. Because both sides use the same local weights, disagreement is evidence about local fit rather than an artifact of comparing different neighborhoods.

Parametric side

Generalized linear models describe a full conditional distribution, not only a fitted mean. The dashboard includes Poisson, negative binomial, beta regression, and related GLM choices.

Nonparametric side

Weighted histograms, conditional kernel estimators, and modified kernel estimators provide model-agnostic local references for the response distribution.

Local weighting

Kernel weights decide which observations belong to the neighborhood of the selected covariate point. Bandwidth controls how narrow or generous that neighborhood is.

Interactive exploration

Observable Framework, D3, Plotly, and WebR make the method browser-based: the model, sliders, scatterplots, PCA view, and comparison plot update together.

Why Bandwidth Matters

The selected conditional point is not a single observation. It is a neighborhood. A small bandwidth asks a very local question, sometimes with too little evidence. A larger bandwidth listens to more observations, but may smooth away the local signal. The dashboard keeps this choice visible.

These displayed weights are normalized across observations, so their total is 1.

When the weighted GLM curve and the data-driven curve disagree, the model may be locally misspecified.

What The Experiments Show

Experiment 1

Locally overdispersed counts

A Poisson model can look reasonable in one region and fail where the data are generated with negative-binomial variation. The local view reveals where the variance assumption breaks.

Experiment 2

Beta responses with changing precision

A beta regression with fixed precision can miss regions where the response becomes much more or less concentrated. The plots point toward a precision parameter that depends on covariates.

Real data

Doctor visits after health reform

The German doctor-visit data are overdispersed overall, but the thesis also checks how Poisson and negative-binomial models behave at particular patient profiles.
